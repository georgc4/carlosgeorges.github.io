Letting the tools judge an RTL change

AutoSilicon gives an agent room to experiment, then uses design tools to decide which changes earned their place.

CARLOS GEORGESPROJECT NOTE · 2026

I have spent a lot of time around design flows, and I wanted to try a simple rule for AI-assisted hardware work: an agent may propose a change, but the tools get the final say. AutoSilicon is a loop that edits SystemVerilog, runs lint and tests, synthesizes the design, and keeps a change only if it improves the measured area and frequency tradeoff without breaking the hard gates.

Edit RTL→Lint + test→Synthesize→Keep or revert

Each experiment is recorded in Git with its metrics. The loop can be stopped by a budget, and regression candidates are reverted. That makes the system more useful than a stream of plausible code: it leaves behind a trace of what was tried and why a version survived.

What the first experiment showed

The included field-oriented-control coprocessor is the initial test case. In 19 autonomous experiments, the best reported result moved synthesized area from 215,164 to 191,487 µm² and estimated Fmax from 171.2 to 187.3 MHz. That is an 11.0% area reduction and a 9.4% Fmax increase relative to its baseline in the documented frontend flow.

Provenance matters here. The FOC baseline itself was created with substantial LLM assistance. I provided direction, specification format, and verification strategy. These results compare against that LLM-designed baseline; they are not a claim that an agent beat a hand-tuned production design.

Still to prove

The frontend loop is working. Backend place-and-route evaluation and external benchmark comparisons are still open. Those steps matter because a good synthesis number does not automatically become a good physical result. That gap is part of what makes this experiment interesting to me.

The repository includes the harness, example design, metrics, and current limitations.
